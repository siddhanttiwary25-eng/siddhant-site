---
title: Trying to solve the May Day Mystery
date: 2026-11-22
summary: Forty-five years of strange newspaper ads, a database, and the theories that didn’t survive it.
---

Every May Day since 1981, the University of Arizona’s student paper has quietly printed an ad that makes absolutely no sense. Imagine a ransom note written by a sixteenth-century theologian who also loved the Eagles: Latin and Greek, coordinates, square roots, and Mao sitting right next to Martin Luther. It’s all signed by a group calling itself the Orphanage. People have spent decades picking at it one clue at a time, and I, with the confidence of someone who has never solved a mystery in his life, decided I would be the one to finally crack it.

My plan was simple (in my head, at least): stop staring at the ads and turn them into data. Working from Bryan Hance’s archive, I gathered and transcribed all 116 ads from 1981 to 2026 and broke each one into pieces, every quote, number, name and doodle, until the whole thing lived in a database I could question. Before testing anything, I wrote down what would count as a real answer, built fake “random” versions of the ads to compare against, and locked away the last four years so I couldn’t bend my theories to fit them.

And at first, it worked. The ads keep a calendar. Whenever one prints a count like “the 494th,” it’s counting the anniversary of the Augsburg Confession, a Lutheran creed signed on June 25, 1530, and every single count is right. My locked predictions for the hidden years got their counts right too. The May Day 1989 ad turned out to be the one later ads point back to most, more than twice what chance would allow. There was even a chain of clues I could follow from start to finish: a deliberate one-letter misprint in a Greek Bible verse, hinted at years later by words like “scordatura” and “Levenshtein distance.” I was sure the treasure map was next. Where did that chain lead? To a single Greek letter.

That was the start of the unraveling. The numbers weren’t a hidden layer of math; they hit famous constants about as often as random numbers do. One theory died, then two, then three, and when the secret message I was certain lived in the misspellings never showed up, I knew this was it. I couldn’t even tell whether one person or a group wrote it. To sweeten my dismay, the honest answer to “is it art or code?” was neither, exactly. The ads look like a collage, rebuilt every year from the same stock of themes inside a ritual they’ve kept for decades.

There was one small surprise left, though. Ads that quote prayers turned out to crack more jokes in their other lines than ads that don’t, almost like comedy built around an earnest core. Worried my AI helpers were just agreeing with each other (two of them were, frankly, cousins), I handed the same blinded text to a model from a completely different company, TypeSafe’s Jev. With a stricter idea of prayer and a looser idea of a joke, it still found the same pattern, for about seventeen cents. It isn’t proof, but it is the one finding that refused to die.

With my head down, I accepted that I hadn’t solved it. What surprised me, though, was what I actually took away. The hard part was never the ads; it was me, and how badly I wanted every pattern to mean something. Most of my favourite ideas died against the fakes, and the few that survived are the only ones I trust. Still, I’ve sealed my predictions for the May 1, 2027 ad. Frankly, I can’t wait to be wrong again.
